# Anatoly + Minimum Effective Dose: Research Report (revised)

Research date: 2026-11-18. Researcher: Opus 5.5. All URLs accessed 2026-11-18.

Scope: what "Anatoly" publicly says about training, and what reviewed research says about low-dose strength training for an adult returning to lifting. These are product principles, not a personal or medical prescription.

**Method note.** I read web pages through a fetch tool that summarizes them, so every source description below is my paraphrase of a summary. None of it is verified verbatim text, and this report contains no direct quotes. Research abstracts came from Europe PMC and some full texts from PMC; PMIDs are given so the original publications can be checked. I watched no videos and installed or bought nothing.

## 1. Who Anatoly is and what the public material says

**Identity.** "Anatoly" is the gym-prank persona of Vladimir (Volodymyr) Shmondenko. This rests on two things:
- The attributed article below.
- The YouTube video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZxtUhe7f2c. Its oEmbed record shows the channel "ANATOLY" at `@vladimirfitness`. The video's public description did not load for me; the parent session verified it separately.

**Cross-link.** The name domain https://www.shmondenkovladimir.com/ is branded Vladimir Shmondenko and links to anatolyfit.com in many places. The parent session points to lines 17, 29 and 54. That cross-link is verified. It is not independent proof that Shmondenko personally wrote everything on anatolyfit.com.

**Primary program source.** https://anatolyfit.com/blog/how-does-anatoly-train-vladimir-shmondenko-s-real-training-routine, dated Sept 14, 2026 and attributed to Shmondenko. According to the article:
- **Philosophy:** planned, phased training rather than maximal effort every week.
- **Lifts:** the squat, bench press and deadlift are the core.
- **Cycles:** heavier phases and higher-volume phases alternate, changing load and volume.
- **Progression:** small load increases, made only while form stays consistent. Some cycles add sets or exercises instead.
- **Frequency and duration:** 3–4 sessions a week, roughly 40–90 minutes each.
- **Sample week:** a plan published for app users that the article explicitly says is not Shmondenko's personal fixed log.

These are the site's own claims about Shmondenko's training. They are not controlled evidence that the approach works for anyone else.

**80/20 and minimum dose.** I found no verified 80/20 or minimum-dose method in the public sources I reviewed. Whether unreviewed Anatoly videos discuss either idea is unknown. The sample program and the stated 40–90 minute sessions are not evidence that Shmondenko advocates tiny-dose training.

## 2. Reviewed research (original publications)

- **S1:** Androulakis-Korakakis, Fisher, Steele. *Sports Med* 2020;50:751-765. [PMID 31797219](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31797219/).
  - Design: systematic review and meta-analysis of resistance-trained men.
  - Finding: single-set programs of moderate-to-heavy loads, done 2–3 times a week for about 8–12 weeks with sets near or to failure, produced modest squat and bench gains.
  - Limits: resistance-trained men, short trials, high effort; deadlift evidence was insufficient.
- **S2:** Spiering et al. *J Strength Cond Res* 2021;35:1449-1458. [PMID 33629972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/).
  - Design: narrative review of maintenance.
  - Finding: younger adults kept strength for months on about one session and one set per exercise a week, provided intensity stayed high. Older adults may need more training to maintain muscle size.
  - Limits: it studied maintenance only, so it doesn't test whether that dose can also produce gains.
- **S3:** Fyfe, Hamilton, Daly. *Sports Med* 2022;52:463-479. [PMID 34822137](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34822137/).
  - Design: narrative review of minimal-dose resistance training across the lifespan.
  - Finding: minimal doses may be more tolerable psychologically than traditional programs.
  - Limits: no pooled dose numbers.
- **S5:** Robinson et al. *Sports Med* 2024;54:2209-2231. [PMID 38970765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/).
  - Design: meta-regression on how close sets went to failure.
  - Finding: strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve (RIR), while hypertrophy favored sets closer to failure.
  - Limits: RIR was estimated from study descriptions; exploratory.
- **S6:** Halperin et al. *Sports Med* 2022;52:377-390. [PMID 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/).
  - Design: meta-analysis of how accurately people predict their remaining reps.
  - Finding: people underestimated remaining reps by about one on average, and were somewhat more accurate nearer failure.
  - Limits: high heterogeneity.
- **S7:** Hickmott et al. *Sports Med Open* 2022;8:9. [PMID 35038063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/).
  - Design: meta-analysis of autoregulated versus fixed load prescription.
  - Finding: similar average strength improvements in the included trials.
  - Limits: trained, mostly young participants; 5–12 weeks; every study had some risk of bias.
- **S8:** Iversen et al. *Sports Med* 2021;51:2079-2095. [PMID 34125411](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/).
  - Design: narrative review of time-efficient training.
  - Finding: it emphasizes weekly volume, multi-joint exercises and exercise-specific warm-ups as ways to save time.
  - Limits: narrative review; mostly untrained or recreational samples.

### What this supports, and what it doesn't
1. **Low doses can work in some conditions.** Short trials of hard single sets produced gains in trained men (S1), and low doses maintained strength in younger adults (S2). Neither finding sets a minimum threshold:
   - S2's maintenance data does not show that one session a week cannot produce gains.
   - S1's 2–3 sessions a week do not show that twice a week is the necessary minimum.
   - The absence of a study is not evidence that an approach fails.

   This review establishes no minimum-frequency threshold.
2. **The variables interact.** Weekly volume, load, effort, practice and adherence all interact. Two full-body sessions give more frequent practice of each lift than two split sessions. Which split fits depends on Evan's actual availability.
3. **Effort context matters.** S1's minimal doses used high-effort sets in trained men. S5 pooled different protocols; it does not validate every combination of low volume and low effort. Neither should be translated automatically into a prescription for a returning lifter.
4. **Prioritizing compound lifts is reasonable** (S8).
5. **Autoregulation.** S7 found similar average strength improvements in short trials of trained participants. That does not prove autoregulation has no benefit in any setting. The idea that it helps by fitting the plan to the day, or by improving adherence, is a product inference. Adherence outcomes were not demonstrated.
6. **Gaps.** Adults returning after a long layoff, especially middle-aged adults, are barely studied in these sources.

## 3. Readiness signals

- **Reps achieved** carry information only alongside a known load, a rep target, a stopping rule and context. If the lifter always stops at the target, the app learns only that the target was reached.
- **An effort report** is not enough on its own either. It needs the load and reps behind it, and self-estimates are noisy (S6).
- **Proposal:** combine reps with one optional, simple effort question to form a noisy signal. This is a design hypothesis, not a validated algorithm. Thresholds should be left to testing.

## 5. Split and time (proposals, no decision)

**Split.** Two full-body sessions mean more frequent practice of each lift. An upper/lower rotation, where the next session is whichever one is due, fits "just tell me what's next." The choice should wait until Evan's real weekly availability is known.

**Time.** Estimate session length from explicit assumptions:
- exercise-specific ramp-up warm-up sets
- rest between sets
- transitions and equipment waits

Don't promise a fixed number of lifts in 30 minutes. Favoring exercise-specific warm-ups is a time-saving design direction (S8), and individualized preparation stays available.

## 6. Proposed product implications (not implemented, not a prescription)

1. **One next action:** the session that is due, its estimated time and a Start button.
2. **An automatic plan** with default load and reps filled in.
3. **Minimal correcting input:** steppers, plus one tap for "as planned."
4. **An optional, simple effort question** instead of a per-set 1–10 RPE, so progression doesn't depend on answering it.
5. **Equipment swaps** to a comparable exercise.
6. **Reliable resume**, so an interrupted session continues cleanly.
7. **Bad-day adjustments kept separate from long-term progression:** a bad day changes today's numbers without rewriting the trend.
8. **Honest copy:** cite only the evidence actually reviewed.
